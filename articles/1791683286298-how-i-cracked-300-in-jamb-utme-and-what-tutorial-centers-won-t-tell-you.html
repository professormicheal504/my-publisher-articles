    The first Big Lie most student believe is: "You Just Need to Read Hard"

    The phrase, "Just read hard, my friend," is one of those popular things Nigerian tutors and parents use to bully students into studying.

    The truth is, reading hard can do more harm than good. When students read hard, it usually means they are staring at thick, glossy textbooks for hours on end. It means sitting with five different 500-page books on their desk and a green highlighter in their hand. It means burning out before the examination day because all they've done is cram.

    JAMB isn't a reading marathon. It's a speed test. And speed tests require a totally different skill set from what you've been taught in secondary school. Your JAMB success depends on how fast you can read and finish calculations to score higher marks within the time limit.

    The writers of JAMB questions aren't gods. They design questions strictly from the syllabus. When you read a textbook like PN Okeke for physics or Ababio for chemistry, more than half of the topics in there will not be on the JAMB examination.

    Guess what? The questions that come out are always from the syllabus. If you haven't printed your syllabus, you're already at a disadvantage since most students who score 250+ read directly from the syllabus.

    Get the appropriate syllabus for your chosen science or art subjects. Print them out on a physical paper and keep them beside your study notes. For instance, if you are studying work, energy and power, do not waste your time trying to derive all theoretical physics formulas in there. The JAMB examiners aren't interested in your problem-solving skills with complex sums.

    But they certainly expect you to understand the basic mechanical calculations and properties of matter. That's one way to maximize your JAMB score by getting familiar with what exactly is on the exam.

    Big Lie 2: "Keep Practicing With the Past Questions"

    In almost every tutorial center in Lagos, Ibadan, Benin or Enugu, I've seen students with thick booklets of past questions in red or blue covers. They are the pride and joy of any JAMB coach because students believe that practicing with past questions is the surest way to score 300+ in JAMB.

    Not only is this ideology misleading, it can cripple your chances of getting a good score. Here's why most students fail when they practice with past questions:

    They memorize the options. Yes, you read that right. Students spend hours memorizing the options on past questions booklets. So, when they arrive at 2017 Chemistry question 26 and see "Option C: Calcium Trioxocarbonate." they instinctively select Option A because they've memorized this question and answer combination from a past questions booklet.

    Memorizing options is a recipe for disaster in a Computer Based Test (CBT) environment where the examiners change question options every year. Here are two reasons why:

    Firstly, most of the past questions booklets sold in local bookshops have wrong answer keys. This happens because the printer misprints the answer key at the back of the booklet. Many students end up memorizing wrong answers precisely because they trust the information in these booklets.

    Secondly, JAMB examiners are expert at twisting and manipulating past questions. Take a question from 2015, change three sentence, alter one word in a definition and you have a brand new question that surprises 90% of JAMB candidates every year.

    So, if you've memorized the entire past questions booklets, you will get excited and click on an option because you've seen it somewhere before. That's the single worst thing you can do in a CBT examination hall.

    What should you do instead? When you're practicing with past questions, always cover the question options with your hand or a sheet of paper. Read the question, try solving it or answering it and then reveal the options. This way, you can pick the right option based on your answer rather than a memorized pattern.

    Also, whenever you get a question wrong, do not look at the correct option and move on. Instead, close the past question booklet and use your biology or chemistry textbook to cross reference the question you got wrong. Understanding why you got a question wrong is the surest way to increase your JAMB score.

    The 60-Question Anchor: Use of English

    Let's get real for a second. Your three main subjects (e.g. physics, chemistry, government etc) have 40 questions each, while use of English has 60 questions. That's a big deal, because use of English makes up a significant percentage of the total 400 marks.

    You cannot score 300+ in JAMB if you have a 45 in use of English.

    There are two sections in use of English that can sabotage your JAMB score if you don't prepare well:


    1. The Prescribed Novel


    Every year, JAMB releases a prescribed novel that all candidates are expected to read. If you are like most Nigerian students, you'll leave it until two weeks before the examination and watch a ten-minute YouTube video on the summary of the novel.

    Don't make the same mistake. Buy the novel, which costs less than ₦1,500, and read it like an interesting bed time story when you're not doing calculations or memorizing biology definitions. Read it a second time and concentrate on the fine print like what the main characters wore, where they came from, who said what to whom and in what order.

    JAMB examiners love asking questions on the finer details of the prescribed novel because they know most students will watch a summarizer video on YouTube and fail to notice those little details. With proper preparation, you can turn those 15 questions on the novel into a sure-fire way of boosting your JAMB score.

    2. Oral English


    Vowel and consonant sounds, stress patterns, rhyming schemes. Most secondary school students in Nigeria don't know much about oral English because bad teachers consider it a waste of time.

    Take the time to learn about the phonetics of the English language and you'll find that oral English questions are some of the easiest ones on the JAMB examination.

    The CBT Hall Panic


    The clock at the top right hand corner of the CBT screen is the single most frightening thing a first-time JAMB candidate will see on examination day. You have 180 questions to answer in 120 minutes, which works out to approximately 40 seconds per question.

    When you are in the examination hall and your heart is beating like you've run a mile, you will panic if you spend 4 minutes on a single math question.

    Once panic sets in, you'll start seeing words that look foreign to you. Guess what? Even the easiest objective questions can trigger a panicked attack if you don't manage your time properly.

    This is how I managed my time in the JAMB CBT hall:


    I divided the examination into three passes. In the first pass, I did not answer any calculation or reading comprehension question. I only clicked on the questions I knew without having to think hard. If a question required a lengthy story comprehension or calculation, I skipped it. After 35 minutes, I had roughly 70 simple questions to click on in the entire 400-mark examination. This boosted my morale massively.

    In the second pass, I went back to the calculation and reading comprehension questions and spent 60 seconds on each one. I did this because I already had 70 easy marks banked on my score.

    The third pass was for the questions I was absolutely clueless about. In JAMB, there is no negative marking, so it pays to make an intelligent guess at questions you don't know. Before you click on the submit button, ensure that you have not left any question blank. If you're really lost, eliminate the two most ridiculous options and make an educated guess between the remaining two.

    Learn the Keyboard Keys


    Many CBT centers in Nigeria use old computers with stiff mouse scroll wheels. You can waste precious minutes clicking on radio buttons to select your answers by learning the keyboard short cuts:

    - Press A, B, C or D to select your answers

    - Press N to move to the next question

    - Press P to go back to the previous question

    - And most importantly, never press S before you're ready to submit your JAMB examination.


    Big Lies You're Probably Being Told About JAMB

    We've all had the experience of doing everything right during our JAMB preparations and pulling everything down on the morning of the examination.

    If your JAMB examination slip says 7:00 am, do not leave your house before 6:00 am. In Nigeria, it takes roughly 45 minutes to drive from one end of the city to the other. You don't want to risk going through road blockades or faulty traffic lights and arrive late at your examination center.

    When you finally arrive at the JAMB examination center and get your number called, do not rush to the computer and type in your registration code.

    Test the mouse and click on a sample option to ensure that the mouse pointer is working. Test the keyboard and look at the computer screen to ensure that it's not flickering or distorting words.

    If something isn't working properly call a staff member's attention immediately before the clock starts ticking. It is your absolute right to call a staff member's attention if you are not comfortable with the computer you're about to use for the JAMB examination.


    Wrap Up

    Scoring above 300 in JAMB is not an exclusive achievement. It doesn't belong to a chosen few. It is a reward for anyone who disciplines him or herself to sit down for two to three hours and test their knowledge with past questions, recognize their weak points and improve on them before the examination day.

    Put your phones aside when it's time to study. Avoid the endless drama of JAMB "runs" and leaked examination questions. Every year, thousands of students lose their JAMB results and examination fees due to shady examination practices.
